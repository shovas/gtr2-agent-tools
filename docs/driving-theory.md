## Racing line

- Outside-inside-outside uses the whole width to make the radius as large as the corner allows. The ideal path has a radius that opens while the car slows, tightens to the apex and opens again on the throttle.
- Late apex: turn-in later, a longer exit radius, more exit speed, less entry speed. The faster the corner, the closer to the geometric line; the slower the corner, the later the apex. An early apex (faster entry, slower exit) is called mostly a beginner error.
- Compound corners and chicanes: sacrifice the line through the early turns to set up the last one, which decides the exit speed.
- The optimum is a minimum-time problem; the minimum-curvature line with a good speed profile is within 0.1 s of a human in Heilmeier et al., and is found by a quadratic programme re-linearised a few times.
- Driver here: matches what the AI benchmark showed (1.5 to 2.5 times our corner radius, full width). Our `--line-opt` is a time-optimal search; the zoned line (slow corners only) agrees with "the slower the corner, the more the line matters", but `--line-opt` on the fast kink at 600 m failed because the follower could not hold it.

## Braking, trail braking, throttle

- Brake hard first, release progressively, with the full release somewhere between turn-in and the apex. Braking loads the front (more front grip, less understeer) and helps the car rotate; releasing the brake lightens the front and lets the rear step out. The slower and tighter the corner, the more trail braking helps; in fast corners the driver is often on the throttle at turn-in. The grip is shared between braking and cornering, so the brake has to come off as the steering goes on (the friction circle).
- Driver here: our pedal is proportional to the speed error plus the kinematic braking term; it holds the brake to the apex in the slow corners (brake on in 17 to 59 percent of the samples around them). The rule "release as the steering goes on" is in the friction-ellipse planner, not in the pedal.

## Tyre model (Magic Formula) and temperature

- The Magic Formula gives the side force Fy = D sin(C arctan(B a - E (B a - arctan(B a)))) from the slip angle a: B stiffness, C shape, D peak, E curvature; the peak force D depends on the vertical load (it grows less than linearly: load sensitivity) and the force falls past the peak slip angle, so more steering beyond it only adds understeer. Fits for autonomous racing cap C at 1.5 and E at 1.1.
- From the Wikipedia pages on Pacejka, slip angle, cornering force, tyre load sensitivity and understeer: the formula is y = D sin(C arctan(B x - E (B x - arctan(B x)))) with each force needing 10 to 20 fitted coefficients (the page calls them fitting constants; the letters' usual meanings are stiffness, shape, peak, curvature); the side force rises about linearly for the first degrees of slip angle, peaks (road tyres at about 6 to 10 degrees of slip, F1 tyres about 3, the peak angle growing with load) and then falls; the friction coefficient falls as the load rises while the maximum side force still rises, roughly with the load to the power 0.7 to 0.9; the side force builds up over a relaxation length of rolling (no value given); a heavier front axle (braking, or a forward weight bias) increases understeer through the same load sensitivity; the combined braking-and-cornering limit is the traction circle (the vector sum of the two forces cannot pass the grip). The MathWorks page for the Magic Formula tyre block lists the coefficient names (PCY1 shape, PDY1 peak friction with PDY2 its load variation, PEY curvature, PKY cornering stiffness, RBY/RCY the combined-slip reduction, PPY the inflation pressure effect) and cites Pacejka's book for the equations; no default values.
- Not found openly: the friction-ellipse weighting formulas and typical coefficient values (they are in the book, whose only free copy found is on a mirror with an invalid TLS certificate).
- Grip needs a temperature window: cold rubber does not conform (graining), hot rubber loses strength (blistering); a harder compound needs more heat. The tyres here run at about 69 C (Soft, 90 C optimum from the tyre files).
- Driver here: the yaw response per steering command is identified live (`--steer-id`); the planner's grip (`--live-lat`, `--live-k`) is the peak D; the spins at a push over about 1.1 are the car going past the peak.

## Path tracking at the limit

- Pure pursuit assumes no side slip (steering from the geometry of the aim point, delta = atan(2 L sin(eta) / Ld)); at the limit it drives wide (Becker et al.). Their fix: take the lateral acceleration pure pursuit asks for, a = 2 v^2 sin(eta) / Ld, and look the steering up in a table built from a single-track tyre model for that speed and acceleration, with an upper bound where no steady state exists: four times lower lateral error than pure pursuit, 0.35 s (5 percent) per lap faster on a 1:10 car; a lookahead that scales with speed (Ld = m + q v) keeps the loop's natural frequency constant.
- Kapania and Gerdes: a feedback law on the lookahead error (x_LA 5 to 20 m) plus a learned steering command per point of the lap, update delta_next = delta - kp e - kd (e - e_previous_point), the error low-pass filtered along the lap (2 Hz, zero phase), gains small; converged in two or three laps on a car at 8 m/s^2; the error can rise again after more laps from noise, so stop learning when the RMS stops falling; the learning only works until the front axle saturates (then the line and speed have to change).
- Driver here: our `--ilc` learns an aim-point shift per 10 m with a gain, a cap and a decay; it did what the paper says (error 1.8 to 1.2 m rms, two to four laps, a rise afterwards, wind-up at the cap). Missing compared with the paper: smoothing of the error along the lap before the update, the neighbour-difference (kd) term, and a stop rule.

### Korf, *You Suck at Racing* (chapter, then what to use)

- **A, Apex:** corner speed v = sqrt(grip times radius): at 1.0 g a 100 ft arc gives 38.6 mph, 200 ft 54.6 mph, 430 ft 80.1 mph; the geometric line (outside, inside, outside, one arc) has the largest radius; the optimal line has a late apex (past halfway): the more power, the tighter the corner, the lower the traction, the later the apex; "when in doubt, drive a late apex" (also safer: slower early, time to fix mistakes). Maps to the AI benchmark: its radii were 1.5 to 2.5 times ours.
- **B, Braking:** threshold braking: reach the peak pressure quickly (a pedal that builds up gently is "unsafe and slow"), tyres slide a little at their best, a smoking tyre has lost grip; trail braking is the gradual release of the brake while turning in, done at the end of the zone, most of the slowing happens in a straight line; applying the brake mid-corner is "fail-braking"; entry speed sets mid-corner speed sets exit speed ("the brake pedal is the go-fast pedal"); engine braking by downshifting moves the brake bias and lengthens the braking distance; ABS is worth keeping.
- **C, Corners:** type 1 slow in fast out (a long straight follows): late apex; type 2 fast in slow out: early apex, threshold braking in a straight line to the apex, then trail brake to finish; type 3 (esses, chicanes, connected corners): sacrifice the exit of the first to set up the next, double apex when two corners are close, one throttle application only; chicanes: straighten as much as possible, emphasis on the exit, be smooth (quick direction changes upset the car); esses mostly need no braking, rhythm matters, the knee-jerk lift when over-speeding causes spins; decreasing-radius corners are the dangerous ones: they need trail braking or a type 2 treatment; increasing-radius corners: get the slow part over quickly then throttle as early and as hard as traction allows. Lifting or braking in a high-speed turn spins the car; some throttle stabilises it.
- **G, Grip:** table of corner speed by radius at 0.8 g (street tyre), 1.0 g (autocross tyre), 1.1 g (R-compound): at 300 ft 59.8, 66.9, 70.2 mph; a 5 percent change in grip speed is worth about 6 s on a 2 minute lap; off the road the grip falls to about 0.6 g, and the radius needed grows (66 mph needs about 500 ft); when the speed is too great for the radius, open the wheel (counter-steer) at once: at the limit more lock does not turn the car more.
- **H, Heel-toe:** blip the throttle at the end of the braking zone, then downshift (not before: over-rev); engine and clutch states matter for balance.
- **N, Nadir:** the minimum-speed point of a corner has an exact speed, position and angle; before it the car only slows (threshold braking, scrubbing, trail braking, or lifting), after it the car only speeds up: "if you have to release the throttle or add steering, you messed up"; too fast at the nadir: open the wheel, take a new nadir, delay the throttle; too slow: coast to the usual throttle point and do not try to make it up (the tyres have grip to spare: keep a little lock and shorten the path). In a type 1 corner the nadir is before the apex, in a type 2 after it.
- **O, Oversteer:** sources: mid-corner braking (load moves forward: if the car is sliding it oversteers: straighten the car while braking, the opened wheel swaps lateral for longitudinal g), trailing-throttle (lifting in a slide), power-on (a spinning tyre loses grip), clutch-pop (shifting mid-corner: engine braking moves mass and bias; do all shifting before the corner), berms and bumps.
- **U, Understeer:** over-braking (all the traction is used for braking: release some to steer), power-on (weight moves back), steering lock (past the optimal slip angle more lock makes it worse: open the wheel or slow down), uphill.
- **R, Rubber:** friction is higher when sliding a little; the grip against slip-angle curve falls much faster below the peak than above it (so err towards more slip); the optimal temperature is hotter than most drivers reach because they drive with too little slip angle; braking heats tyres; the friction coefficient falls as the load rises (wider tyres, stiffer suspension spread the load).
- **S, T, V:** simulation advice (hardware, the original rFactor is still usable), track maps with notes for each corner, vehicle basics (not used).

### IRDC manual (pages 13 to 16 and the glossary)

- The circle of traction: a tyre has 100 percent of its grip to split between braking, cornering and accelerating; weight changes with acceleration, braking, turning, abruptness and aerodynamics; transitions should be as smooth as possible; every driver input should be as quick as the car's balance allows and as smooth as its stability needs ("stable platform").
- Threshold braking: as much brake as possible at the start, then a gradual release to transition to turning; stomping upsets the car (load moves forward too abruptly); a locked wheel loses about 30 percent of its grip; trail braking (overlapping brake release and turn-in) is an advanced technique.
- Terms: "transition" (feathering the throttle between brake and throttle), the efficient slip angle 6 to 8 degrees (5 to 10 range), unwind the steering gradually to straighten, curbing at the apex and exit where cars put two wheels off, apex too early is the common mistake (not enough road at the exit), oversteer: steer into the skid and do not lift.

### Pacejka, *Tyre and Vehicle Dynamics* (chapter and section numbers of the 2nd edition)

- **1.2 to 1.3 (tyre and axle characteristics, handling, stability):** the handling diagram (steer angle against lateral acceleration) shows the understeer gradient and the limit; non-linear steady-state cornering (1.3.3), the moment method (1.3.5, Milliken's MMM diagram: at the limit of steady cornering the car can no longer produce a positive yaw moment).
- **1.3.4, the vehicle at braking or driving (page 51):** longitudinal force moves load to the front under braking: the front cornering stiffness rises and the rear one falls: the understeer gradient falls (about 0.05 times ax/g for the book's example car): a braking car turns in more readily and is closer to oversteer; the longitudinal tyre force reduces the peak side force (friction ellipse); at hard braking up to wheel lock stability and steerability can deteriorate severely (chapter 3). A vehicle in the extreme condition can be brought to the desired attitude by briefly inducing a large brake or drive slip at the rear while the wheel is turned back.
- **3.2 brush model:** side force against slip angle from tread elements, the interaction of lateral and longitudinal slip (3.2.3), camber and turn slip (3.2.4); section 3.4: vehicle stability at braking up to wheel lock.
- **4.3 Magic Formula:** model description (4.3.1) and the full set of equations (4.3.2, pages 172 to 184); appendix 3 lists the parameter values; semi-empirical combined-slip weightings in 4.2.
- **5, 7, 8, 9:** transient tyre response: relaxation length, the lag of side force behind slip angle (relevant to steering lag and the follower), ABS braking.

## Plan: how the findings go into the driver

Rules for every item: from data, no fixed zones, no aids, no on-screen indicators, human-plausible inputs (`RackModel`, pedal slew) unchanged; one change per test, 10-lap sessions against the committed baseline (Imola 4, Maserati, `track_params.json` entry: 10 of 10 clean, best clean 1:55.9, typical clean 1:56.5 to 1:57.5, clean laps repeat to about 0.1 s once the push is capped); adopt when the clean count is not lower and the median clean lap is at least 0.3 s faster (lap-to-lap noise 0.3 to 0.5 s), otherwise record the result in `docs/analog-driving.md` and leave the option off; times in m:ss.s next to the AI benchmark (1:48.0 to 1:48.6) and the target (1:50.8).

1. **Friction circle on the pedal** (IRDC circle of traction, Korf B and O, Pacejka 1.3.4): `--brake-circle F` is written (default off, untested): the brake is limited to sqrt(1 - (lateral used / lateral limit)^2) of the maximum. Test F = 1.0, then 0.8. Success: no spin at the corner entries of 1469 m and 2600 m, the clean lap not slower.
2. **Open the wheel at the limit** (Korf G, U "steering lock", Pacejka side force beyond the peak): when the measured front grip fraction (`tel["wheels"][0..1]["grip"]`) falls below about 0.5 while the lateral error grows, hold or reduce the steering command instead of adding lock, and let the speed come down. Test as `--open-wheel`. Success: fewer off-road excursions, no more 15 m-off-the-road stops.
3. **Brake pedal shape: quick peak, then taper** (Korf B, IRDC threshold braking): the pedal rises to the planned pressure within about 0.25 s of the brake point, holds, and tapers to zero by the nadir (type 1) or the apex (type 2 and decreasing radius) instead of following the planner's slope; the kinematic brake keeps the slope as the target of the hold. Success: shorter braking distance at the same entry speed (the telemetry comparison with the AI recording shows where the AI brakes later).
4. **Downshift policy** (Korf B engine braking, O clutch-pop, H heel-toe): downshift only at the end of the braking zone and never in the corner; with the pedal in the model a blip is possible but the first test is just the policy: no gear change while the lateral acceleration is over about half the limit. Success: no rear step-out on entry (the spins' traces show downshifts 5 to 1 through the slide).
5. **Nadir rule** (Korf N): after the minimum-speed point the throttle never falls below a maintenance value and the steering never increases (lift or lock added past the nadir counts as an error); before it, only brake or lift. Success: smoother exits, fewer late corrections; the throttle trace after the nadir is monotonic.
6. **Corner types and apex placement** (Korf A, C): classify each corner from the planner's profile (a long straight after: type 1, late apex, nadir before the apex; a long straight before and a slow section after: type 2, early apex; chicanes: straighten, emphasise the exit) and give the line optimiser an apex-position preference per type; this can be done offline first by comparing the optimiser's apex positions and radii with the AI's per corner (the recorded AI lap in `scripts\gtr2_ui\runs\ai_imola_mc12.csv`). Success: the optimised radii reach the AI's (1.5 to 2.5 times ours) in the slow corners without track-limit events.
7. **Grip model with load sensitivity** (Korf R, G, Pacejka load sensitivity, the Wikipedia pages): replace `a_lat (1 + k v^2)` by a fitted table of lateral acceleration against speed (data-derived: the AI recording gives p90 16 to 18 m/s^2 at 20 to 40 m/s and 10 to 13 above 50 m/s; our own logs give the same bins) and check it offline with the lap estimator before any lap. Success: the planned corner speeds near the AI's without raising the push.
8. **More slip angle for the tyre temperature** (Korf R, IRDC slip angle 6 to 8 degrees): the front grip fraction stays near 1.0 in our logs and the tyres sit at 69 C against 90 C: aim at a front grip fraction of about 0.85 to 0.95 mid-corner (a little slide) instead of 1.0, watch the tyre temperature in the result (`tyre_temp`); the grip-per-temperature curve says the gain is large if the temperature reaches the window. Test after items 1 and 2 make a slide safe.
9. **ILC refinements** (Kapania and Gerdes): low-pass the error along the lap before the update, add the neighbour-difference term, stop learning when the RMS stops falling. The current `--ilc` has the gain, cap, decay and incident clearing.
10. **Slip-aware steering map** (Becker et al.): steering from the lateral acceleration the aim point asks for through the yaw gain measured per speed, clamped at the grip limit. Earlier `--steer-live` (measured yaw response scaling) did not help; try after item 2.
11. **Pacejka's equations, if a model is wanted:** Magic Formula coefficients for a car tyre (4.3.2, appendix 3) and the brush-model combined-slip weighting (3.2.3) to build a single-track model of the Maserati for the follower simulator (grip 15 to 17 reproduces the game); relaxation length (chapter 7) to model the steering lag. Only worth it if the offline simulator is to be used to design a controller.
